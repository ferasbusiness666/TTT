The Price of Being Black in Suburbia

Navigating Black identity in a town that was never built with you in mind — and building your own belonging anyway.

LT
Liya G. Tadele
Editor-in-Chief · Nov 14, 2025 · 7 min read
cover photo — 16:9
A quiet cul-de-sac in the suburbs where the writer grew up. Photo: TTT Photo Desk

The first time someone asked me “what are you?” I was eight years old, standing in line for the water fountain. I didn't have an answer that fit on a worksheet. In a town where every house looked like the one beside it, I learned early that I was the variable — the thing that made the picture look different.

Suburbia sells itself as neutral. Manicured lawns, good schools, a certain quiet. But neutral is never neutral for everyone. For a Black teenager, the suburb is a place where you are simultaneously hyper-visible and completely unseen — the only one in the honors class, the one the security guard follows, the one whose hair is a “can I touch it” exhibit.

Hyper-visible, and invisible

There's a specific kind of tired that comes from being a representative. When you're the only one, every opinion you have becomes The Black Opinion. Every bad day becomes evidence. You start editing yourself before you've even spoken — a survival skill nobody teaches you but everybody expects.

“You learn to shrink and to shine at the same time. It's a full-time job nobody hired you for.”

My parents moved here for the schools. That's the story every family tells, and it's true. But the fine print — the part nobody warns you about — is that access and belonging are not the same thing. I had access to everything. Belonging was a different application, and the review process never seemed to end.

Finding the others

Here's the part that saved me: I found the others. Not many — you could count us on two hands — but enough. We found each other in the back of the bus, in the one AP class, in a group chat that started as homework help and became something closer to a lifeline.

That's really why Tadele Teen Talks exists. Not to complain, and not to perform trauma for people who want to feel bad for a minute and move on. It exists because when you're the only one in the room, a platform full of your people is the room you wish you'd had.

What I'd tell my eight-year-old self

You are not the variable. You were never the thing that made the picture wrong. The frame was too small. Go find a bigger one — or better, build it, and leave the door open behind you.
